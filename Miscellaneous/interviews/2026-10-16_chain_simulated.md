## Full answers

(AI-simulated persona: Priya Nair, pseudonym. Not a real person.)

## Q1. What is your role, and how many members does your club or chain serve?
I'm area manager for three Pulse Fitness clubs: Raffles Place in the CBD, Jurong East in a mall, and Punggol, which opened about 18 months ago. Pulse has 12 clubs nationally, and my three hold roughly 4,000 members. About 40% are on 12-month contracts, 25% on 6-month, and 35% month-to-month. Head office sets prices and promotions, so I run operations and retention, not pricing. My bonus is tied to net member growth and retention, so I watch churn every month.

## Q2. How do you find out today that a member is about to leave? What do you do about it?
Honestly, mostly I don't, until they walk to the desk or send the cancellation email. Head office gives me a churn dashboard, but it tells me who left, not who is about to. What I do myself is pull "visits in the last 14 days" and look at the names that have gone quiet. Each club's consultant has maybe 5 to 6 hours a week for retention calls, so we can only call a few dozen people a week. If someone asks to cancel on month-to-month, we can offer the standard save: one month at 20% off. It works for maybe a third of them, if I'm guessing.

## Q3. A regular who came twice a week drops by a fifth or more this month. Would you act on that? After how long?
A fifth is noise. Someone going from 8 visits to 6 in a month could just have been travelling or had a busy week. I'd not act on that alone. If it's a drop of half or more, or two weeks in a row with zero visits from someone who normally comes, then yes. For month-to-month members I'd act after about two weeks of silence. For contract members I'm more relaxed, because they usually don't leave until renewal. If a tool flagged every 20% dip, my staff would drown.

## Q4. When are new members most at risk: the first month, the first three months, or later?
First 30 days, no question. My rule of thumb is that if they haven't come 8 times in the first month, they're gone by month three. The 90-day retention number matters, but it's really decided in the first four weeks. After month three the habit is either formed or not. Later churn exists, but it's different, mostly life events, moving, or renewal decisions.

## Q5. How low does attendance have to fall before you treat someone as disengaged?
For a regular, I'd say no visit in 14 days when they normally come weekly, or a drop of 50% or more against their own usual pattern for three to four weeks. For a new member, it's tighter: if they've had under 2 visits in the first 2 weeks I'm already worried. It depends on the person's own baseline, not an absolute number. Someone who comes once a month isn't disengaged at once a month.

## Q6. Do members on 6- or 12-month contracts leave at renewal? How far ahead of the end date would you reach out?
Yes, contract members mostly leave at renewal, not mid-contract. A mid-contract drop in visits is the best early warning, though, because it predicts that they won't renew. I'd reach out about 6 to 8 weeks before the end date for 12-month, and about 3 to 4 weeks for 6-month. Earlier than that it feels like a sales pitch. And I never discount them mid-contract.

## Q7. When an instructor leaves, do their regulars follow them out? Can you give an example? How long after does it show?
Yes, it can be bad. Our star HIIT instructor at Raffles Place left for a boutique studio. Class attendance fell about 40% within 3 weeks. Over the next two months, maybe 10% of her regulars cancelled. The rest moved to other classes, but only if we called them quickly. The drop in attendance shows within 2 to 3 weeks, the cancellations show over 1 to 2 months. So there is a window, and speed matters.

## Q8. Same question for a cancelled class or time slot.
Smaller effect, but real. If a 6:30pm class is cancelled, the people who only came for that slot just stop coming, and they don't tell us. Lunchtime classes at the CBD are the sensitive ones, because office workers have a fixed window. I'd say it shows within 2 to 4 weeks as attendance drop, and fewer of them cancel outright than with an instructor. The fix is a service message, not a promotion: "your class moved to this slot." That's allowed without marketing consent.

## Q9. For a new member who is slipping, what works best: a call, a free PT session, something else?
A call from a named consultant, in the first two weeks, works best. A free PT session also works but costs real staff time and the trainers resent it if it's too many. What I find most effective is booking them into a first-class or a buddy session so they have an appointment. Email is useless here, under 20% open rates. I would do the call first and only escalate to a PT session for those who pick up and say they're unsure what to do.

## Q10. For a long-time regular who is fading?
Different approach. These people know the place, so a hard sell feels wrong. A front-desk chat if they still visit, or a personal call from someone who knows them. Ask what changed: new job, injury, instructor left? Often it's the class or instructor. A discount is the wrong tool for a long-timer, they'll just take it and leave later. I'd offer a schedule change, a new class suggestion, or a check-in.

## Q11. Do social offers (bring a friend, group challenges) work for members who joined with a friend?
Yes, I think so, particularly at Punggol with the young couples. If they joined together and one drops off, the other usually follows within a month or two. A bring-a-friend or paired challenge helps. But I'd say it depends: for a CBD office worker who joined alone, a group challenge is irrelevant. I don't have rigorous numbers on this, it's more my observation.

## Q12. When do you give discounts, and to whom do you never give them?
Discounts need head-office approval, except one standard save: one month at 20% off for month-to-month members who ask to cancel. I never discount annual members mid-contract. It breaks the contract terms and upsets members who paid full price, and they do find out. I'd also avoid discounting someone who is still attending regularly and just complaining, since it trains them to ask.

## Q13. Which channel gets answered: SMS, email, phone or in person? Does that depend on age or offer?
Email open rates are poor, under 20%. SMS is read but rarely answered. Calls from a named consultant work best but cost staff time. In person at the front desk is best for members who still visit. App push notifications exist at head office level only, I can't use them. Age matters a bit: younger members at Punggol respond to SMS and messaging, older members and families at Jurong pick up calls more. For a time-limited offer, SMS is fine. For anything personal, call.

## Q14. How often can you contact the same member before it backfires? (Give a number: once per how many days? How many offers in, say, 3 months?)
My rule is no more than one proactive contact per member every 14 days, and I'd say no more than 2 offers in 3 months. Beyond that people feel hounded and unsubscribe, or cancel to make it stop. For new members in the first 30 days I'd allow a bit more, maybe a touch every week. These are my gut numbers, I haven't tested them formally.

## Q15. Are there members you would never spend a paid offer on?
Yes. Annual and 6-month members mid-contract. Members who are still highly engaged, because they'd stay anyway. Anyone with no marketing consent on record, for a promotional offer. And serial discount-seekers, who've already taken a save offer before and came back to cancel. Paid offers should go where they change the outcome, not where the member was staying anyway.

## Q16. Any rule you must follow: data protection, head office policy, contract terms?
Three. PDPA: marketing messages need recorded consent, while service messages like class changes or check-ins are allowed. Head office policy: discounts need approval apart from the one standard save offer. Contract terms: no mid-contract discounts for annual members. If a tool suggests something that contradicts these, my team will stop trusting it immediately.

## Q17. If a tool gave your staff a weekly call list with reasons, what would make you trust it, and what would make you ignore it?
I'd trust it if it shows reasons I can check, like "visits down 60% over 3 weeks, class instructor left", and lets me override. It also has to be right about the members I already know: if it flags three people I know are at risk, I'll believe the fourth. I'd ignore it if it floods staff with low-risk members. My consultants have 5 to 6 hours a week, so the list should be maybe 15 to 25 names per club, ranked. And I'd ignore it instantly if it contradicts head office promo rules, for example proposing a discount for an annual member.

## Q18. Anything we should have asked?
Ask about outcomes: how do we know a call actually worked? We rarely track it properly. Also staff turnover: when a consultant leaves, relationships and notes go with them, so the tool should record what was tried. And ask about renewal timing, since contract members cluster at January and after promotions, which makes call workload spiky.
