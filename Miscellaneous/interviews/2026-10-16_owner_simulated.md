## Full answers

AI-simulated persona: Lim Wei Ming (pseudonym), owner-manager of IronWorks Fitness, Tampines. Not a real person.

## Q1. What is your role, and how many members does your club or chain serve?
I'm the owner and manager of IronWorks Fitness in Tampines. Single site, opened twelve years ago, and before that I was a PT at a chain for eight years. I still train clients two mornings a week because I like it and because it keeps me on the floor. We have about 1,300 active members. Roughly 45% are on 12-month contracts, 20% on 6-month, and 35% month-to-month, and month-to-month pays about thirty dollars more. Staff-wise I have one full-time front desk manager, two part-timers, four freelance PTs and six freelance instructors for spin, HIIT, body pump, yoga and zumba.

## Q2. How do you find out today that a member is about to leave? What do you do about it?
Honestly, mostly I find out too late. The software shows check-ins and contract end dates, and once a month I export to Excel and have a look. Mostly it's the front desk who notices: "Eh, where's Mr Tan, I haven't seen him." Or the member walks up and says they want to cancel, or the month-to-month just stops paying. When we do notice, the front desk sends a WhatsApp or SMS, "Hi, haven't seen you, everything okay?" For older members and long-time ones, I or the front desk manager will call. There's no system. No CRM. It's whoever remembers.

## Q3. A regular who came twice a week drops by a fifth or more this month. Would you act on that? After how long?
No, not for a fifth. Twice a week going to maybe 1.6 times, that's nothing. That's one sick week, one work trip, one public holiday. People are not machines. I would only start to care if it's a clear gap, like three weeks in a row of nothing for someone who normally comes twice a week. Maybe two weeks if it's a new member. A one-month dip of 20%, if a tool flagged that I would ignore it, and my staff would too after the third time. Too noisy.

## Q4. When are new members most at risk: the first month, the first three months, or later?
First three months, and the real danger is weeks 3 to 8. In the first month they are still excited, they paid, they come. Then the novelty drops, work gets busy, and if they have not built a habit by week six or so, they are gone in spirit, even if the contract still runs. I'd say if someone isn't coming at least once a week by the end of the second month, they are very unlikely to stay beyond the contract. January joiners are the worst. Maybe half of them have disappeared by March. They join for the resolution, not for the gym.

## Q5. How low does attendance have to fall before you treat someone as disengaged?
For someone who used to come regularly, I'd say no visit for 3 weeks, or coming less than once in two weeks for a month. For a new member, tighter: zero visits in 10 to 14 days after the first two weeks, or fewer than 4 visits in the first month. But it depends. Chinese New Year, school holidays, NS call-up, they can disappear for two weeks and come back fine. A regular of five years who skips a week, I don't call that disengaged, I call that life.

## Q6. Do members on 6- or 12-month contracts leave at renewal? How far ahead of the end date would you reach out?
Yes, renewal is the exit door. People don't leave in the middle because they are locked in, so they just stop coming and then don't renew. For 12-month I'd reach out about 6 weeks before the end, and 4 weeks for 6-month. Not earlier, because they will forget, and not later because by then they have already decided. I prefer a personal message or a call at that time, not a blast. And a good number of the ones who are not using the gym will tell you honestly, "I don't come, so I won't renew." Can't fix that with a message.

## Q7. When an instructor leaves, do their regulars follow them out? Can you give an example? How long after does it show?
Yes, definitely, especially for classes. Two years ago my most popular spin instructor moved to a competitor. About 15 to 20 of her regulars left within two months, and some followed her directly. The first drop was within two or three weeks, those were the very loyal ones, and the rest trickled out over the next month or so. It hurt because spin riders are quite sticky to the person, not the bike. Freelancers are the risk, because they have no loyalty to my brand, only to their own following.

## Q8. Same question for a cancelled class or time slot.
Also yes, but it's softer. If I cancel a 7pm HIIT slot, the people who only come for that slot, usually working adults, will try another time maybe once or twice. If it doesn't fit their schedule, they drift. I'd say a third of them quietly fade within a month or two. It's worse for the early-morning uncles and aunties because they are creatures of routine. Change the time by an hour and some will just stop. The effect is slower than with an instructor leaving, maybe 4 to 8 weeks.

## Q9. For a new member who is slipping, what works best: a call, a free PT session, something else?
For a new member, I like a free PT session or a free class pass, something that gets them into the building and gives them a person to meet. A PT session works best because they build a connection with a trainer, and then they feel a bit bad to skip. A call alone is okay but young people don't pick up, they prefer WhatsApp. A WhatsApp from a real person, "Hi, this is Coach Ah Kiat, I saw you haven't come, want to try a session on me?" is better than a promo message. The cost to me is one hour of a freelance PT, about SGD 30 to 40 that I pay them. Much cheaper than losing a member worth maybe 100 a month.

## Q10. For a long-time regular who is fading?
A phone call, from me or the front desk manager who knows them. Not a promo. You ask, "Eh, long time no see, everything okay? Injury? Work?" Many times there's a reason, a knee problem, a new baby, a new job, and then we can help, like suggest a lighter class, or change their time, or pause their membership. Offering a discount to a five-year regular is almost insulting. If they are fading and I don't know why, that I'd like to know first.

## Q11. Do social offers (bring a friend, group challenges) work for members who joined with a friend?
Sometimes. If they joined as a pair and both are still coming, bring-a-friend is nice, it keeps them anchored. But if one of the pair stopped coming, the other usually follows within a month, so you must reach the one who stopped. Group challenges, I've done 8-week transformation ones, work for the keen types but not for the quiet ones who just want to be left alone. I would not push a group challenge on someone who is shy or older. I don't have numbers for friends really. It's a feeling, I notice friends leave together.

## Q12. When do you give discounts, and to whom do you never give them?
Rarely. I believe discounts train members to wait for promos. I did "one month at 20% off" for month-to-month members a few times. Results were mixed: some stayed, but some took the discount and left the next month anyway. I'd consider it for a month-to-month member who says money is the problem, in writing or in person, not for everybody. I never give it to my long-term regulars who are still active, because why reward threatening and punish loyalty, and I never give it to people who ask for it as a negotiation trick at the counter. I'd rather give value: a free PT session, a class pass.

## Q13. Which channel gets answered: SMS, email, phone or in person? Does that depend on age or offer?
WhatsApp for under 40, they answer quickly. SMS they ignore, honestly. Email almost nobody reads, maybe 1 in 10. For the 50-plus members, a phone call works best and also in person at the counter, they like to talk. For long-time members of 3 years or more, a call from someone they know. For an offer like a free session, WhatsApp is fine. For anything about renewal or money, I prefer a personal call or at least a personal WhatsApp, not a template.

## Q14. How often can you contact the same member before it backfires? (Give a number: once per how many days? How many offers in, say, 3 months?)
For me, no more than once every 14 days for anything marketing-like, and I'd say max 2 offers in 3 months. For older members, even less, maybe one contact a month. If you message the same person three times in a month they get annoyed and then they treat us as spam. If they replied, the clock resets and it's a conversation, that's different. If they haven't replied after two messages, stop and let a human call once, then leave them alone.

## Q15. Are there members you would never spend a paid offer on?
Yes. Members who are happily active already, why pay to retain someone who isn't leaving? Also the serial promo-takers, people who always cancel and come back for a discount. And members who already told us they're leaving for a clear reason, like relocating overseas or a medical issue, no point. And honestly the ones with a bad record, like those who abuse the facilities or are rude to staff, I won't spend money to keep them. My budget is limited, a few hundred dollars a month for this kind of thing, so I need to aim it at people who are on the fence, not the sure-stay or sure-go.

## Q16. Any rule you must follow: data protection, head office policy, contract terms?
No head office, I'm independent, but PDPA is real. I need consent for marketing messages, and I must respect opt-outs. For marketing SMS and calls to numbers on the Do Not Call registry, we need clear consent or an existing relationship in the right context, and I'm not 100% sure where the line is between a service message and a marketing message, so I'm careful. A renewal reminder is probably okay as service, a discount offer is marketing. Contract terms also matter: I can't change someone's contract price or freeze rules unfairly, it must be in writing. And I don't want member data in some tool that I can't explain to the PDPC if they ask.

## Q17. If a tool gave your staff a weekly call list with reasons, what would make you trust it, and what would make you ignore it?
Trust: the reason is in plain words, like "was coming 2 times a week, no visit in 24 days, contract ends in 5 weeks." The list is short, 10 to 15 names a week, not 80, because my front desk has maybe 2 to 3 hours a week for this, and each call takes 5 to 10 minutes. It must tell us what to say or offer, and be cheap. And it should get better when we say "this one was wrong." Ignore: if it flags my loyal five-year regulars because they skipped a week at Chinese New Year, or if it can't explain itself, or the same names show up every week, or it suggests a discount for everybody. If the first two weeks of the list are junk, my staff will stop looking, and I won't force them. Also if it needs a lot of data entry from my staff.

## Q18. Anything we should have asked?
Ask about the staff side. My freelancers are the real asset and the real risk, so a tool that tells me "these 20 members all come for this one instructor" is more useful to me than one that scores individual members. Also ask about what happens after the call: did it work, did they come back within 3 weeks? If the tool doesn't track that, I won't know whether it was worth any money. And ask about seasonality, January and Chinese New Year and the school holidays all distort the numbers, so a tool that doesn't know the calendar will cry wolf.
